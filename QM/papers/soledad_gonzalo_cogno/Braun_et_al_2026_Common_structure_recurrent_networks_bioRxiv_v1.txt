ABSTRACT 12 
Neural sequences, characterized by neurons or groups of neurons that ﬁre one after the 13 
other, have been observed in multiple brain regions, across species, and are known to 14 
underlie a diversity of brain functions. To ﬂexibly support behaviour and cognition, neural 15 
sequences exhibit much variability in properties like their temporal width, baseline, and 16 
peak ﬁring rate. Despite this variability  and the central role that sequences play in 17 
supporting brain function , a framework that explains how ﬂexible sequences are 18 
generated and dynamically maintained within a circuit is still missing. Here we go beyond 19 
traditional approaches that investigate a one-to-one relationship between  network 20 
connectivity and speciﬁc sequential dynamics . I nstead, we train recurrent neural 21 
network models  to generate a repertoire of sequential dynamics and characterize the 22 
obtained connectivity matrices . We found that diberent connectivity matrices can 23 
generate the same neural sequence, yet all connectivity matrices that generate a speciﬁc 24 
sequence share a common  connectivity proﬁle , deﬁned here as the average weight 25 
between pairs of neurons as a function of their distance in the sequence ordering . It is 26 
the connectivity proﬁle, as opposed to the connectivity matrix, that serves as a ﬁngerprint 27 
of the sequential dynamics  and shapes the network response to perturbations of the 28 
neural activity . Our model predictions were consistent with results obtained from 29 
experimental data recorded across brain regions and across species. Finally, w e 30 
demonstrated that neural sequences can facilitate and constrain the formation of a large 31 
repertoire of sequences in downstream brain regions , with  the potential of acting as 32 
scabolds for a wide range of computations. Altogether, our results explain how network 33 
connectivity can generate a diversity of neural sequences across circuits and how those 34 
sequences can be ﬂexibly adapted. Our framework reveals sequences as a common  35 
algorithm to support brain function across brain regions and species. 36

INTRODUCTION 37 
Sequences of neural activity are characterized by neurons or groups of neurons that ﬁre 38 
one after the other. Neural sequences have been reported in many brain regions [1] such 39 
as the hippocampus  [2-7] and the visual cortex [8-10], and also across species , for 40 
example in rodents [3, 11, 12], bats [13], zebraﬁsh [14], turtle [15] and humans [16, 17]. 41 
Moreover, neural sequences are known to underlie multiple brain functions , including 42 
working memory [7, 18], songbird learning [19, 20], and the representation of time [5, 7, 43 
11, 13] . The ubiquity of sequential activity across brain regions, species , and brain 44 
functions suggests that neural sequences constitute a universal pattern of neural activity 45 
that can be dynamically adapted to support ﬂexible computations. 46 
Decades of theoretical work have demonstrated that neural sequences can be generated 47 
in a neural network with predeﬁned connectivity, such as feed-forward networks [21-23], 48 
synﬁre chains [24-26], or continuous attractor network s [27-30]. Sequences can also 49 
emerge in the absence of prewired connectivity, due to plasticity mechanisms [31, 32] or 50 
training algorithms [33]. These existing models have primarily focused on how a given 51 
network architecture supports one speciﬁc neural sequence . However, a theoretical 52 
framework that explains how a diversity of sequences can be generated , ﬂexibly 53 
modiﬁed, and dynamically maintained in neural circuits is still missing. 54 
In this study,  we constructed recurrent neural network models that were trained to 55 
generate sequences of activity  with a wide range of properties. We characterized the 56 
connectivity matrices that gave rise to those sequences, and their associated 57 
connectivity proﬁle s, deﬁned  as the average weight between pairs of neurons as a 58 
function of their distance in the sequence ordering . The model  predictions were  59 
consistent with connectivity inferred from experimental data [12, 13] . By pushing the 60 
dynamics out of the sequential regime through local and global perturbations of the  61 
network activity, we investigated how the network response was controlled by features of 62 
the connectivity proﬁle . Finally, we found that neural sequences can facilitate the 63 
formation of a large repertoire of sequences in downstream brain regions . We 64 
demonstrate that those sequences are constrained by the properties of the upstream 65 
sequence and its corresponding connectivity proﬁle. 66

Changes in the connectivity proﬁle map changes in the properties of the neural 117 
sequences 118 
We next investigated the relationship between features of the connectivity proﬁle and 119 
properties of the target sequences.  First, we observed that sequences emerged from an 120 
asymmetric connectivity proﬁle (Figure 2A left ). This asymmetry supports the temporal 121 
progression of the sequence by enabling the propagation of localized activity, or ‘bump 122 
of activity ’, throughout the network.  The asymmetry increased with increasing obset  123 
values (Figure 2A, Supp . 3A). Furthermore, when we increased the width of the 124 
sequences the range of the connectivity proﬁle, deﬁned as the diberence between its 125 
maximum and its minimum, decreased (Figure 2B, Supp . 3B). Finally, increasing the 126 
sequence amplitude preserved the asymmetry in the connectivity proﬁle while it 127 
increased its range  (Figure 2C). Interestingly, an oscillation  of increasing frequency 128 
appeared (Figure 2C, Supp. 3C). These results predict which features the connectivity 129 
proﬁle should have in order to support speciﬁc target sequences. Moreover, they show 130 
that smooth changes in the neural sequences are supported by gradual changes in the 131 
connectivity proﬁle (Supp. 4, Supp. Material). 132 
Previous work has shown that RNN dynamics are shaped not only by global features of 133 
the connectivity matrix, but also by higher order statistics [37-42]. We next quantiﬁed the 134 
presence of connectivity motifs in the connectivity matrices that emerge from  training 135 
(Supp. 5A). We found that increasing the target sequences’ obset, width, or amplitude led 136 
to a higher abundance of reciprocal connectivity motifs  (Supp. 5B). Interestingly, we did 137 
not ﬁnd any divergent, convergent nor chain connectivity  motif, suggesting that these 138 
motifs, and in particular chain motifs, are not strictly necessary for supporting sequences 139 
of activity (Supp. 5B). These results extend the connection between connectivity proﬁles 140 
and target sequences by demonstrating that changes in the target sequences are 141 
accompanied by gradual changes in the connectivity motifs. 142

Network connectivity supports, and constrains, ﬂexible sequences in downstream 241 
brain regions 242 
Sequences of neural activity can act as scabolds that facilitate computations [7, 11, 13, 243 
18, 19] or the formation of new patterns of activity in downstream regions [9, 12, 43]. This 244 
is especially relevant given that sequential activity has been reported in multiple brain 245 
areas [1, 5, 12, 19, 44]. We next investigated how sequences of activity shape sequential 246 
dynamics in d ownstream region s. First, w e simulated an ‘upstream network ’ that 247 
consisted of 501 units and that exhibited sequential activity  (Figure 5A). Next, we used 248 
the activity in the upstream network as input to a ‘downstream network’, which was 249 
composed of 10 units. All upstream neurons were connected to all downstream neurons 250 
(Figure 5A). We trained those weights to enforce sequential activity in the downstream 251 
network. Finally, we considered one speciﬁc sequence in the upstream network 252 
(obtained after FORCE training as we did in previous sections - parameters: amplitude = 253 
0.5, width = 1 s, obset = 0.3) and asked whether the downstream circuit could learn 254 
sequences with varying properties using the predeﬁned upstream sequence as input  255 
(Supp. 10A, Figure 5B). 256 
We found that the training was successful as long as the sequence in the downstream 257 
region was as wide as, or wider than, the sequence in the upstream region (Figure 5C-E). 258 
In addition, we found that the same upstream sequence could generate a downstream 259 
sequence with a wide range of amplitude and obset values (Figure 5F, G, Supp. 10B, C). 260 
Next, we investigated the impact of perturbations in the upstream sequence  on the 261 
generation of sequences in downstream areas. As expected, w e found  that if the 262 
perturbation is such that the activity in the upstream layer is disrupted, then  the activity 263 
in the downstream layer is disrupted too (Supp. 10D, E). This result suggests that  the 264 
scabolding role is constrained to sequences unfolding in an uninterrupted manner. Our 265 
results can be understood analytically (Supp. Material): since the downstream network 266

DISCUSSION 273 
The brain is known to generate a big repertoire of sequences of neural activity across 274 
multiple brain regions, ranging from visual, auditory, and somatosensory areas, to 275 
subcortical regions such as the striatum and the hippocampal formation  [1, 7, 9, 11-14, 276 
19, 45, 46]. To understand how a variety of sequences can be generated and dynamically 277 
modiﬁed within and across circuits, we went beyond traditional approaches that focused 278 
on the one -to-one mapping between a predeﬁned connectivity matrix and neural 279 
sequences with ﬁxed properties [24, 27, 31, 33] . Instead, we trained RNNs to generate 280 
neural sequences with a wide range of properties and investigated the connectivity 281 
matrices that support those sequences. We found that diberent connectivity matrices 282 
can support the same sequence, yet all  those connectivity matrices share a common 283 
connectivity proﬁle. In addition, the connectivity proﬁle changes smoothly as properties 284 
of the sequences vary. We showed that sequences reset upon perturbations, and that 285 
these resets are controlled by the connectivity proﬁle. Finally, we quantiﬁed how the 286 
connectivity proﬁle can facilitate, and constrain, the formation of a big repertoire of 287 
sequences in a downstream area. 288 
In this work, we found that multiple recurrent connectivity matrices can generate the 289 
same sequential dynamics. More speciﬁcally, we found that the connectivity matrix can 290 
be decomposed into a structured component, and an unstructured residual. Importantly, 291 
only the structured component is constrained by the target sequences, while the residual 292 
can vary without abecting the sequential activity of the network. These ﬁndings reveal a 293 
many-to-one relationship between recurrent connectivity and sequential dynamics. This 294 
degeneracy is not speciﬁc to our study but a general feature of recurrent networks [47, 295 
48]. For example, in low -rank RNNs the structured low-dimensional component of the 296 
connectivity matrix determines the dynamics, while the unstructured component is  297 
unconstrained [49, 50]. Additionally, recent studies show that the structured component 298 
can be implemented in diberent ways and still give rise to equivalent dynamics  [51-53]. 299 
Together, these results suggest that even though the many-to-one relationship between 300 
connectivity and dynamics is a robust feature of RNNs, networks that implement the 301 
same dynamics share a common underlying structure. In the case of sequential activity 302 
considered here, this shared structure is the connectivity proﬁle, a circulant component 303 
embedded within the recurrent connectivity. 304

Our ﬁndings show that the peak ﬁring rate, width, and baseline activity of the sequences 305 
can be dynamically modiﬁed by adjusting properties of the connectivity proﬁle such as 306 
its degree of asymmetry, its oscillatory frequency and its maximum and minimum values. 307 
These speciﬁc modiﬁcations may enable the formation of new sequences on the ﬂy 308 
without the need for substantial circuit rewiring. This ﬂexibility may extend to other 309 
properties of the sequences too. For example, it has been shown that increasing the 310 
weights between excitatory assemblies shortens the duration of a sequence, and  311 
increasing the weights from excitatory to inhibitory assemblies extends it [23]. While 312 
network connectivity plays a key role in shaping sequential dynamics, other mechanisms 313 
in the brain may also contribute to adjusting the properties of sequential activity, such as 314 
the magnitude and temporal structure of external input [54] or the brain’s temperature 315 
[55]. 316 
The degeneracy of the connectivity matrix, together with the generation of ﬂexible 317 
sequences that are enabled by gradual changes in the connectivity proﬁle, may facilitate 318 
the brain’s ability to adapt the sequential  neural dynamics to varying behaviours and 319 
cognitive demands. They may also support the coexistence of diberent dynamics and 320 
codes within the same network. For example, sequences may need to quickly change 321 
according to the context or choices in a decision-making task [3, 18], or based on external 322 
stimuli [7]. Furthermore, much of the information encoded in a network, such as  323 
contextual cues or memory traces of past events, should remain stable regardless of 324 
ongoing computations supported by the sequences. The degeneracy of the connectivity 325 
matrices may have the computational advantage of preserving stable information 326 
representations while enabling ﬂexible and behaviour-dependent sequences. 327 
Our results provide testable predictions for connectome datasets. The degeneracy of the 328 
connectivity matrix suggests that simply comparing  network connectivity with 329 
connectome data may be misleading . Instead, it is the structured component of the 330 
connectivity matrix, which in this work is captured by the connectivity proﬁle, that should 331 
be compared to experimental data. Because our ﬁndings are not restricted to any speciﬁc 332 
brain region or species, our model predictions could potentially be  tested on 333 
connectome data collected from diberent animal models, such as rodent [56], ﬂy [57-59] 334 
and zebraﬁsh [60]. All these species are known to exhibit sequences of neural activity.  335 
The many-to-one relationship between recurrent connectivity and sequential dynamics  336 
may pose challenges when trying to infer connectivity from neural activity data [61-64]. 337 
Such inference is generally dibicult even with sophisticated methods, because as we 338 
show here, the dynamics constrain the underlying connectivity only weakly  [63, 64] . 339 
However, knowing that the connectivity must contain a low -rank circulant component 340 
provides prior information for the inference. Furthermore, a previous work shows that the 341 
inference may be facilitated in the presence of perturbations [63]. Along this line of work, 342

Previous studies have shown that sequences of activity might serve as a scabold for local 345 
and downstream computations. For example, activity sequences in the mouse striatum 346
